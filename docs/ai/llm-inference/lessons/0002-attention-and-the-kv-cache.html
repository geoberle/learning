Lesson 02 · About 5 minutes

How earlier words help

You already know the sequence: process the newest token, reuse saved work from earlier tokens, then predict what comes next. Let’s pause inside “process the newest token.” Why does it need information from earlier text?

1. The last word is not enough

Read this unfinished sentence:

Suppose the model has just generated it and is processing it to predict the next token. The word it alone tells us little. Looking back at dog, chased, and ball gives useful context for possible continuations.

There is no single guaranteed continuation. Our question is smaller: how can information from those earlier tokens reach the calculation for “it”?

2. Bring some information forward

Imagine a card for each token containing information the model has computed about it. While processing it, the model can combine information from these cards. Some cards can contribute more than others.

The name for this operation is attention: combining information from accessible tokens, with a computed share for each contribution.

Attention is one part of processing a token. More calculations follow before the model produces its next-token prediction. Source: attention and model processing.

3. What does a “share” mean?

Use a smaller, invented calculation. Imagine that three cards contain just one number each: 2, 10, and 4. These are practice numbers, not encodings of the sentence above.

If the shares are 10%, 60%, and 30%, take those fractions of the three numbers and add them:

Each share is called an attention weight. In this example the weights are 10%, 60%, and 30%. They total 100%.

Notice what changed: we started with three separate pieces of information and obtained one combined result. Giving the second card more weight makes its information contribute more. These weights describe contributions from input tokens, not the probabilities of possible next words.

4. Back to the model

A real card contains a list of numbers rather than our single practice number. Such a list is called a vector. The model applies the same mixing idea to the numbers in those lists. You do not need vector notation to understand this step.

The shares are calculated by the model, not supplied by a person. We will open that calculation in the next lesson. For now, keep the two ingredients separate: information to combine and how much each contribution counts. Source: attention’s weighted combination.

This connects to your understanding of the cache: earlier information can remain available while a new token makes fresh use of it. We will explain exactly what is saved after the mixing idea feels clear. Source: reusing earlier results.

Change the shares yourself

The three cards still contain 2, 10, and 4. The first mixture gives most weight to 10. The second gives most weight to 4. Before switching, predict whether the result will rise or fall.

The buttons change only the shares. The information on the cards stays the same.

Why does the result change?

It falls from 7.4 to 5.0. We take less from the larger number, 10, and more from the smaller number, 4. A different mixture does not require changing the original cards.
